### Chapter 1

- Desire seems formulated in a similar way to many of Akerman's films, in that it is unattainable, but deeply yearned for, like the dream of a lover that is shattered upon waking
- Marcel describes his mother's leaving him with the phrase "gone back downstairs" (redescendue), which is a very evocative phrase
- "my mother [...] watched [my father] with a tender respect, but not so intently as to try to penetrate the mystery of his superior qualities"
  - This reminded me of the conversation between Simon and Ariane in La captive, where Simon is frustrated that Ariane does not seem to understand him, and he cannot understand her. The idea of a "mystery" that cannot be penetrated, and the frustration that comes with that, seems very relevant to Akerman's work.
- Proust directly references the idea that we don't have the true version of someone in our head, it's a version of them that we have constructed based on our own experiences and perceptions, which is very relevant to Akerman's work, especially in terms of the way she captures subjectivity and the interiority of her characters
- On the value of constraints in creating art: "Like good poets forced by the tyranny of rhyme to find their most beautiful lines"
- I'm very struct by the phrase "there is no answer" (Il n'y a pas de réponse) when Marcel gives his mother a letter to try and make her come upstairs
- Proust and Akerman compete for most mum-obssessed person
- "In me, too, many things have been destroyed that I thought were bound to last forever and new ones have formed that have given birth to new sorrows and joys which I could not have foreseen then, just as the old ones have become difficult for me to understand"
  - I love this way of capturing the way that we constantly reconstruct our ideas, memories, experiences, and selves
- "when my anxieties were soothed, I no longer understood them"
- Proust describes how the first novel we read is something so singular, I feel like the same can be said of new formal structures in cinema, like Akerman's work
  - Imagine watching a new film without having seen Boss Baby
- Marcel's mother reads to him, but elides the love scenes, thus replacing love with a kind of profound mystery
  - This brings to mind Akerman's interactions with Bilderverbot: elision of the image to mystify it
  - It also reminds me of the way Calum Neill describes Lacan's *objet petit a* as a kind of black hole that cannot be perceived directly, but is only known through its effects on surrounding objects
- The whole Madeleine section is absurdly beautiful, and is also very Akerman-esque:
  - "The past is hidden outside the realm of our intelligence and beyond its reach, in some material object [...] which we do not suspect."
  -  But at the very instant when the mouthful of tea mixed with cake crumbs touched my palate, I quivered, attentive to the extraordinary thing that was happening inside me. A delicious pleasure had invaded me, isolated me, without my having any notion as to its cause. It had immediately rendered the vicissitudes of life unimportant to me, its disasters innocuous, its brevity illusory, acting in the same way that love acts, by filling me with a precious essence: or rather this essence was not merely inside me, it was me."
  - I have spent some time considering whether the Madeleine is a postmemorial object in the same sense as Akerman's grandmother's diary from *Tomorrow We Move*, and I think there's an important distinction, which is hinted at in Proust's book as well:
    - "I drink a second mouthful, in which I find nothing more than in the first, a third that gives me a little less than the second. It is time for me to stop, the virtue of the drink seems to be diminishing. Clearly, the truth I am seeking is not in the drink, but in me. The drink has awoken it in me, but does not know this truth, and can do no more than repeat indefinitely, with less and less force, this same testimony which I do not know how to interpret and which I want at least to be able to ask of it again and find again, intact, available to me, soon, for a decisive clarification."
    - The madeleine and tea are not the source of the memory, but rather a trigger for it. The memory is already within Marcel, and the madeleine and tea simply bring it to the surface. This is different from Akerman's grandmother's diary, which is a postmemorial object that carries the weight of historical trauma and memory, and serves as a means of transmitting that memory to the next generation.
    - Considering another example, the road from *Sud*, which is a kind of postmemorial landscape, I would say that the road itself is still in some sense an embodiment of the memory, as a physical space shaped by the events that took place there, and which carries the weight of that history.
    - This seems an important distinction to make, and I think it is something that could be explored further in relation to Akerman's work:
      - To what extent do the objects and landscapes serve as postmemorial objects/landscapes, and to what extent do they serve as triggers for the memory that is already within the characters or the audience?
  - The madeleine sequence has another vital aspect to it: the way that it identifies "seeking" memories as more of an act of creation than of recollection:
    - "Such grave uncertainty, whenever the mind feels overtaken by itself; when it, the seeker, is also the obscure country where it must seek and where all its baggage will be nothing to it. Seek? Not only that: create. It is face-to-face with something that does not yet exist and that only it can accomplish, then bring into its light."
    - This is an interesting through which to view the way Akerman's works that deal in memory are active processes that work within the viewer, rather than passive presentations of a pre-existing memory.
  - "And suddenly the memory appeared." (Et tout d'un coup le souvenir m'est apparu.)
    - Interesting that the indirect object is implicit in the English translation.
  
### Chapter 2

- "happy with a peace that brings only an increase of anxiety and with a quality of the prosaic that serves as a great reservoir of poetry to one who passes through it without having lived in it."
- "an edifice occupying a space with, so to speak, four dimensions – the fourth being time – extending over the centuries its nave which, from bay to bay, from chapel to chapel, seemed to vanquish and penetrative not only a few yards but epoch after epoch from which it emerged victorious"
- Cursed thought: Time in this book is very weird because it can slow right down and so much thought can occur in the tiny gaps between actions. This is just like action sequences in anime where time slows all the way down, and the characters have dueling monologues (talk blocks) where they consider what is occurring in depth. So yeah, Proust is basically writing a battle shōnen anime about memory.
- The Narrator is constantly considering how he can give or receive kisses
- "I am turning a corner... but... I am doing so in my heart"
- "[none of those engravings evoke] the feeling that makes us not consider a thing a spectacle, but believe in it as in a creature without equivalent, none of them holds in subjection an entire profound part of my life"
- "You have a lovely soul, of a rare quality, an artist's nature, don't ever let it go without what it needs"
- "she had taken some insignificant remark of my father's, had worked it delicately, turned it, given it a precious appellation, and enchasing it with one of her glances of the finest water, tinged with humility and gratitude, had given it back changed into an artistic jewel, into something 'completely exquisite'"
- "The kitched-maid was an abstract entity, a permanent institution whose invariable set of attributes assured her a sort of continuity and identity, through the succession of temporary forms in which she was incarnated."
