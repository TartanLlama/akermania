- The "fourth person singular" is a concept I struggle with, but here are my notes based on some deeper readings:
  - It seems to build on concepts from Deleuze (who himself built it from a poem by Lawrence Ferlinghetti)
  - Narboni invokes the phrase *ça montre* (it shows) in relation to Je, tu, il, elle, which is a term from Lacan by way of Freud ("Le Ça" being the French term for the Id)
  - Lacan's *ça montre* appears to refer to something showing itself beyond the level of conscious subjectivity. In Narboni's case, this is likely tied to the cinema image itself, where what matters is less who sees than the fact that something shows itself.
  - Narboni's Blanchot quote ("part de l'événement que son accomplissement ne peut réaliser") is from the book The Space of Literature, specifically the section "The Secret of double Death" in the chapter on "Rilke and Death's Demand" (Rilke being the poet Rainer Maria Rilke)
    - The full quote (translated) is "There is much to suggest that the movement by which he purifies death, by taking away its random character, forced Rilke to incorporate this randomness in its essence, to close it upon its absolute indeterminacy, so that instead of being only an improper and untimely event, death becomes, at the heart of its invisibility, that which is not even an event, that which is not accomplished, yet which is there, the part of this event which its accomplishment cannot realize."
    - Apparently Rilke desired to make death something individual, something cultivated throughout one's life, and Blanchot's argument is that personalising death so completely paradoxically makes it indeterminate. Death ceases to be a simple event that happens at a particular moment, and instead becomes something that can never be fully experienced or realised as an event.
    - The one who dies is, in a sense, the only person for whom death matters absolutely, yet also the one who can never experience it as a completed experience. Death becomes "the part of the event which its accomplishment cannot realize".
    - You could therefore see death as an event that is always looming or approaching, but which can never become fully present or complete itself as experience.
  - Deleuze's conceptialisation of "fourth person" also bases some of its ideas on Blanchot's analysis of death and impersonal events.
    - For Deleuze, singularities and events are primary, while subjects and identities emerge secondarily from them.
    - The "fourth person" refers not to another grammatical person beyond I, you, he/she, but to an impersonal field of events existing prior to subjectivity.
    - It is the depersonalised, pure event; not something that happens to an already-formed subject, but something from which the subject itself emerges.
  - Nabroni also uses the term "temps du moins-que-parfait", which is a play on "pluperfect tense" (plus-que-parfait). 
    - You could read "temps du moins-que-parfait" as a kind of "less-than-perfect tense": a temporality that cannot be fully completed or concluded.
    -  An event that is not fully realised, but nonetheless persists or remains in some sense.
  - Putting all of this together, Nabroni's use of "fourth person singular" seems to be about the way cinematic images can point toward an impersonal level of experience that precedes subjectivity. Rather than showing what "I" see, what "you" see, or what happens to a particular individual, the image can reveal anonymous events: a *ça montre* in which something shows itself without belonging to any stable subject. In this sense, the "fourth person singular" names the event itself, not yet organised into a person, and perhaps never fully completed within one.
  - Particularly for Je, tu, il, elle, it seems to describe the way the film moves through the pronouns of its title without allowing any of them to become definitive. Julie is successively "I", "you", companion to "him", and lover of "her", yet the film repeatedly shifts attention away from identities and towards durations, encounters, movements and desires themselves. The "fourth person singular" would then name this impersonal level of experience.
